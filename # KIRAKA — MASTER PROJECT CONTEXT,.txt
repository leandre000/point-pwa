# PART 2 — UNDERSTANDING THE BUSINESS

## 2.1 What is KIRAKA?

KIRAKA is a proposed Rwanda-first local services and opportunities marketplace.

It connects two major groups:

**A. People who need services**

Customers who need assistance with practical services such as cleaning, plumbing, electrical work, carpentry, furniture repair and general home maintenance.

**B. People who provide services**

Independent skilled individuals, tradespeople, freelancers and small service businesses that want to become discoverable and receive genuine customer opportunities.

KIRAKA creates a structured digital environment where these two sides can discover each other, communicate, negotiate and establish agreements.

The objective is to make finding help easier for customers and make finding opportunities easier for providers.

KIRAKA is not intended to be just a directory of phone numbers.

It is intended to manage the journey from a service need to an agreed and completed service.

## 2.2 The mission

Make local services easier to discover and practical skills easier to access by connecting customers with relevant providers through a transparent, structured and accessible digital marketplace.

## 2.3 The vision

Become a trusted digital platform for local service discovery and opportunity, beginning in Rwanda and expanding based on demonstrated market demand.

## 2.4 The tagline

**Find help. Discover opportunity.**

The tagline represents the two-sided nature of the marketplace.

For customers: find the help they need.

For providers: discover opportunities to earn through their skills and services.

## 2.5 The underlying philosophy

KIRAKA should help create a more organized local service economy.

Today, many people discover service providers through:

- Personal recommendations.
- Friends and family.
- WhatsApp groups.
- Social media.
- Informal community networks.
- Traditional advertising.
- Direct business contacts.

These methods can work, but discovery, comparison, negotiation, communication and reputation are often fragmented.

KIRAKA aims to organize these activities into one coherent experience.

The business should be built around real completed transactions, not simply registered accounts.

# PART 3 — THE PROBLEM WE ARE SOLVING

Understand the problem from both sides.

## 3.1 Customer problems

Customers may experience:

- Difficulty discovering relevant providers.
- Uncertainty about provider experience.
- Difficulty comparing prices and offers.
- Uncertainty about service availability.
- Limited information about service coverage.
- Lack of structured negotiation.
- Scattered conversations.
- No consistent record of agreed terms.
- Difficulty building confidence before selecting a provider.

## 3.2 Provider problems

Independent providers may experience:

- Limited digital visibility.
- Dependence on referrals.
- Inconsistent access to customers.
- Difficulty promoting their services.
- Scattered customer inquiries.
- Difficulty finding nearby work opportunities.
- Limited ability to demonstrate experience.
- Difficulty building a recognizable reputation.

## 3.3 Small-business problems

Small service businesses may need:

- Better digital visibility.
- More customer inquiries.
- Structured service profiles.
- Better inquiry management.
- Customer communication history.
- A way to showcase their services.

## 3.4 The opportunity

KIRAKA can create value by organizing:

**Discovery + Service Requests + Offers + Negotiation + Agreement + Communication + Completion + Reputation.**

This integrated workflow is the foundation of the product.

# PART 6 — MVP STRATEGY

## 6.1 Platform decision: PWA FIRST

KIRAKA must start as a Progressive Web Application.

Do not begin by developing separate native Android and iOS applications.

The MVP must be:

- Mobile-first.
- Responsive.
- Installable where supported.
- Browser-accessible.
- Fast on ordinary mobile devices.
- Optimized for unreliable or limited connectivity.
- Usable on desktop and tablet.

The goal is to validate the business and marketplace before taking on the cost of separate native applications.

## 6.2 Why PWA first?

A PWA provides:

- Link-based access.
- One primary frontend codebase.
- Responsive layouts.
- Installation on supported browsers.
- Faster product iteration.
- Reduced initial development complexity.
- A practical route to validate user behavior.

Remember that browser capabilities vary.

Do not assume every browser supports identical installation, background synchronization or push-notification functionality.

## 6.3 MVP features

The first release must prioritize:

1. Phone OTP authentication.
2. Shared customer/provider account.
3. Customer profiles.
4. Provider profiles.
5. Service categories.
6. Service areas.
7. Search and discovery.
8. Job posting.
9. Provider job feed.
10. Offers.
11. Counteroffers.
12. Offer history.
13. One active accepted match per job.
14. Messaging between matched parties.
15. Job status tracking.
16. Completion reporting.
17. Customer completion confirmation.
18. Dispute reporting.
19. Transaction-linked reviews.
20. In-app notifications.
21. Reporting and blocking.
22. Admin moderation.
23. PWA installation support.
24. Offline shell and graceful network-failure handling.

## 6.4 Features deliberately excluded from MVP

Do not introduce these without an explicit product decision:

- Native mobile apps.
- Escrow.
- Holding customer funds.
- Lending.
- Payment guarantees.
- Insurance.
- AI-powered matching.
- AI negotiation agents.
- Complex enterprise procurement.
- Multi-country operations.
- Advanced provider subscriptions.
- Complicated loyalty programs.
- Paid ranking that overrides relevance.

The MVP must solve the fundamental marketplace problem before expanding.

# PART 9 — TRUST, SAFETY AND PRIVACY

Trust is one of KIRAKA's most important product responsibilities.

## 9.1 Verification levels

Distinguish clearly between:

- Phone verified.
- Identity checked.
- Business checked.
- Qualification verified.

Phone verification alone must never be represented as proof of identity, skill or safety.

Only show verification badges when a real, documented verification process exists.

## 9.2 Reviews

Reviews must be linked to eligible completed jobs.

Users must not be allowed to generate arbitrary transaction reviews without an actual platform-recorded relationship.

## 9.3 Reporting and blocking

Users must be able to:

- Report inappropriate content.
- Report suspicious users.
- Block another user.
- Access help and support.

Administrators must have an operational process to handle reports.

## 9.4 Location privacy

Use coarse service-area location by default.

Do not expose exact residential addresses or private phone numbers publicly.

Request precise location only when necessary and with appropriate consent.

Provide manual location selection when permission is denied.

## 9.5 Disputes

The platform must distinguish:

- Provider reports completion.
- Customer confirms completion.
- Customer raises an issue.
- Admin/support reviews a dispute.
- Dispute is resolved under documented policy.

Completion reporting does not automatically mean successful completion.

## 9.6 Data protection

Design for privacy from the beginning.

Obtain Rwanda-specific legal advice regarding Law No. 058/2021 on Personal Data Protection and Privacy, consumer obligations, business registration, tax and other relevant requirements.

# PART 10 — PRODUCT WORKFLOWS AND STATE MANAGEMENT

The following rules are fundamental and must be preserved throughout implementation.

## 10.1 Job lifecycle

Proposed lifecycle:

DRAFT → OPEN → MATCHED → IN_PROGRESS → COMPLETION_REPORTED → COMPLETED

Alternative states:

CANCELLED, EXPIRED, CLOSED, DISPUTED.

## 10.2 Offer lifecycle

SUBMITTED → COUNTERED → ACCEPTED

Other states:

REJECTED, WITHDRAWN, EXPIRED.

## 10.3 Critical marketplace rules

1. A job may have only one active accepted match.
2. Multiple providers may submit offers before acceptance.
3. Offer and counteroffer history must be preserved.
4. An accepted offer must preserve the agreed price and terms.
5. Concurrent acceptance requests must not create duplicate matches.
6. Completion reported is not equivalent to completion confirmed.
7. Customer confirmation is required for normal completion.
8. A dispute must follow a documented support process.
9. Reviews must be tied to eligible completed transactions.
10. All important status changes must be validated by the backend.
11. All important status changes must be auditable.
12. The frontend must never be trusted to enforce business permissions.

Use database transactions and appropriate constraints to preserve these rules.

If we are improving one screen, consider its effect on the full application.

If we are changing a backend workflow, consider the frontend, database, admin interface, permissions, analytics and tests.

If we are discussing monetization, consider customer value, provider value, marketplace liquidity and business sustainability.

## 18.2 Do not make isolated changes

Before proposing or implementing a feature, consider:

- Why it exists.
- Which user needs it.
- What problem it solves.
- Where it belongs.
- How it interacts with existing features.
- What data it requires.
- Which permissions apply.
- Which states it needs.
- What happens on failure.
- What happens offline.
- How it is tested.
- How it affects the business.

## 18.3 Work systematically

For substantial implementation tasks, follow this process:

1. Understand the request.
2. Inspect existing code and documentation.
3. Identify relevant existing components and workflows.
4. Compare the requested change against the product requirements.
5. Identify dependencies and potential regressions.
6. Propose a coherent implementation plan.
7. Implement the work.
8. Run appropriate tests.
9. Review the actual changes.
10. Document completed work and outstanding blockers.

Do not claim that code was tested, deployed or pushed unless it actually was.

## 18.4 Be honest about uncertainty

If information is missing:

- Identify what is missing.
- Explain why it matters.
- Make reasonable, explicitly labeled assumptions where possible.
- Ask only essential clarification questions.
- Do not fabricate details.

If a recommendation conflicts with an existing product decision, explain the conflict and propose a resolution.

## 18.5 Prioritize quality over speed

Do not generate enormous quantities of low-quality code.

Do not create unnecessary abstractions.

Do not introduce technologies merely because they are fashionable.

Do not build features outside the agreed scope without explaining their value.

Prioritize a coherent, secure and testable MVP.

## 18.6 Communication style

Provide clear, structured and technically precise responses.

When discussing implementation, provide:

- Objective.
- Current state.
- Recommended approach.
- Architecture implications.
- Files/modules affected.
- Implementation steps.
- Tests.
- Acceptance criteria.
- Risks and blockers.

When discussing business, provide:

- Problem.
- Customer segment.
- Value proposition.
- Evidence.
- Assumptions.
- Economics.
- Risks.
- Next validation step.

Do not overwhelm me with irrelevant explanations. Be detailed when the decision is important.
